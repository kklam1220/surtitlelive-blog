If your non-English show is going to the Edinburgh Fringe Festival, you may start with a simple sentence:

**We need English subtitles.**

That sentence is useful for audiences. It is not always precise enough for production.

A venue may hear "captions" and think about D/deaf access. A translator may hear "surtitles" and think about prepared live translation. A producer may hear "subtitles" and imagine a video-style file. A stage manager may only care whether someone can cue the text live when the actor skips.

Everyone may be using reasonable words.

They may still be planning different things.

The problem is not whether you call them subtitles, surtitles, or captions.

**The problem is building the wrong workflow because the word was never defined.**

This article is not another full guide to adding English surtitles to a Fringe show. For the practical setup, read [How to Add English Surtitles to a Non-English Show at the Edinburgh Fringe Festival](/blog/9-english-surtitles-non-english-show-fringe/).

This article is about the decision that should happen before setup:

**What kind of text are you actually asking for?**

## The word is not the workflow

"Subtitles" is what many audiences search for.

"Surtitles" is what many theatre and opera teams prepare.

"Captions" are often an access commitment, not just a translation file.

"Projection" and "mobile viewer" are delivery methods. They do not tell you what the text contains.

Mixing these up can put the wrong text, on the wrong screen, for the wrong audience.

For a non-English production at the Edinburgh Fringe Festival, the most common starting point is prepared English surtitles: a reviewed English translation, broken into readable live cues, advanced by an operator in time with the performance.

But that is not the same as accessibility captioning.

And neither term tells you whether the audience should read the text on a shared screen, in Projection Mode, or on their own phones.

That is why the better first question is not "Do we need subtitles?"

It is:

**Are we translating language, providing audio access, choosing a delivery method, or doing more than one of those at the same time?**

## The common terminology traps

The danger is not that one word is morally correct and another is wrong. The danger is that vague language creates vague planning.

| If the team says... | But means... | The risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "We need subtitles." | English translation for a live non-English show | Someone may prepare a video-style file instead of cueable live text. |
| "We need captions." | English surtitles for foreign-language dialogue | The venue may assume an accessibility captioning requirement with sound cues and speaker information. |
| "We have surtitles." | Translation-only text | D/deaf or hard-of-hearing access may still be incomplete. |
| "We will project the subtitles." | A full text workflow | The team may solve screen placement before solving translation, cue timing, or operator recovery. |
| "Audiences can use phones." | A finished mobile workflow | The team may forget language selection, QR entry, testing, or live cue control. |
| "PowerPoint is fine." | A live surtitle cockpit | The operator may be trapped when actors skip, pause, or the cue list changes. |

This is the real terminology trap: the word sounds like a decision, but the production still has to define the content, delivery, and live operation.

## Subtitles: useful audience language, weak production spec

For audiences, "English subtitles available" is often clear.

It tells them the show will not leave them outside the language. It is also closer to the language many audience members are likely to use: English subtitles for theatre, subtitles for a non-English play, subtitles for Fringe Festival shows.

So there is nothing wrong with using "subtitles" in audience-facing copy if that is the clearest promise.

But inside the production team, "subtitles" is often too broad.

It may suggest a fixed video file. It may suggest bottom-of-screen text. It may ignore the fact that theatre is live. A live show needs text that can wait, jump, recover, and disappear when needed.

For a scripted non-English Fringe show, the production spec should usually be more precise:

**prepared English surtitles, cued live by an operator.**

That one sentence already tells the translator, stage manager, venue, and software provider much more than "we need subtitles."

## Surtitles: prepared live translation

Surtitles are prepared text shown during a live performance. In theatre and opera, the term usually points to translation: the show is spoken or sung in one language, while the audience reads another.

The word originally suggests text above the stage, but the modern workflow is broader. Surtitles may appear on a theatre screen, a projector, a monitor, or an audience device.

The essential point is not where the text sits.

The essential point is that it is prepared for live performance.

That means the text has to be:

- short enough to read in time
- reviewed for meaning and rhythm
- split into cues that match performance beats
- controlled by someone who can follow the actors
- recoverable when the show moves differently from rehearsal

That is why a surtitle workflow is not just a translation workflow. It is a live delivery workflow.

## Captions: access text, not just translated lines

Captions do different work.

The Edinburgh Festival Fringe captioning guidance distinguishes captioning from surtitles. In that guidance, surtitles are associated with English translation for foreign-language productions, while captioning can support D/deaf or hard-of-hearing audiences and may include more than dialogue.

That difference matters.

An English surtitle may translate what a character says.

A caption may also need to identify who is speaking, indicate off-stage voices, music, sound effects, or other audio information that a non-hearing audience needs in order to understand the performance.

So if a non-English company says "we need captions" but only prepares translated dialogue, it may not have met the access need.

And if a company says "we need surtitles" when the venue is asking about captioning access, the venue may not know whether the production has planned for sound information, speaker labels, or caption review.

The practical question is simple:

**Are we translating the spoken language, providing access to sound as well as speech, or both?**

That question should be answered before anyone builds the cue list.

## Projection and mobile are delivery decisions

A screen is not a content strategy.

Projection can be excellent when the room supports it. A shared screen can feel clean and theatrical. Everyone reads the same text. No one needs to hold a phone.

But the Edinburgh Fringe Festival is not one room with one technical setup. A small touring team may face short tech time, awkward sightlines, limited equipment, or a venue that was never designed around surtitles.

That is why mobile viewing can be practical.

With a mobile viewer, audience members scan a QR code or open a link, choose an enabled language, and read the prepared text in their browser. No app install is required.

But "mobile" does not automatically solve the text problem either. The company still has to decide:

- which language tracks are enabled
- whether the English text is translation, captioning, or both
- whether the original language should remain visible as a separate option
- whether Projection Mode should run alongside mobile viewing
- what the operator needs to cue live

Projection and mobile are delivery choices.

They become useful only after the team defines the text.

## A clearer production request

Instead of asking a venue or tool provider:

**Can we have subtitles?**

Ask something closer to this:

**We need prepared English surtitles for a live non-English show. The text will be cued by an operator. We may need Projection Mode, mobile viewer access, or both. If captioning access is required, we need to plan sound and speaker information separately.**

That request is longer.

It is also much harder to misunderstand.

It tells the venue that the show is live. It tells the translator that the text must be cueable. It tells the stage manager that someone has to operate the text. It tells the producer that access captions and translation surtitles are related but not identical.

Most importantly, it keeps the team from discovering the difference only during tech.

## The poster can be simple. The workflow cannot.

Use "English subtitles" when that is what audiences understand.

Use "surtitles" when planning prepared live translation.

Use "captions" when planning access text for sound as well as speech.

Use "Projection Mode" or "mobile viewer" when deciding how the audience will receive the text.

The word does not need to be perfect in every poster.

The workflow does.
